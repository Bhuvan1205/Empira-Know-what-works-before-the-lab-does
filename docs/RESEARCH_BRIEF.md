# Empira targets the empty order-time window

Empira's defensible position is timing plus breadth, not the modeling idea. It predicts resistance from existing EHR data at the moment a culture is ordered, which is roughly **2.7 days before blood-culture susceptibility results normally arrive** ([Tabak 2018, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6258864)). Every rapid AST product found still needs a specimen, and most need a positive blood culture first. No commercial stewardship tool checked publicly advertises patient-level resistance prediction. The academic field, by contrast, is crowded. Stanford's own Corbin et al. 2022 already used LightGBM on Stanford EHR data ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9053259)). Yelin 2019 and Kanjilal 2020 already showed urine mismatch reductions of 18–42% versus clinicians ([Yelin PDF](https://kishony.technion.ac.il/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Yelin_et_al-2019-Nature_Medicine.pdf); [CIDRAP](https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/antimicrobial-stewardship/researchers-create-algorithm-improve-antibiotic-uti-treatment)). An Israeli order-set tool has already shown real-world mismatch reductions ([npj Digit Med 2025 record](https://library.kab.ac.ug/Record/doaj-art-879433afd04c4b459be0b61ee17a3a1d/Description)). Empira's urine result is resistant suggestions in **8.0% of cases vs 11.8% for clinicians**, about a 32% relative reduction. That figure comes from the project's own internal analysis, not a published source, and it falls in the same range as prior work. What is credibly new is the breadth (28 antibiotics across urine, blood and respiratory cultures), the use of the newly public ARMD dataset, and a 2022–2024 temporal test that stresses drift after COVID. The pitch should present Empira as an empiric-decision layer that complements rapid diagnostics and feeds existing stewardship workflows. It should also name prospective or silent-mode validation as the gap to close.

The dataset contains **283,715 unique adult patients and 751,075 culture records** from Stanford Health Care's two academic-affiliated hospitals, covering **Dec 1999 to Feb 2024**. Of the cultures, **50.0% are urine, 38.8% blood and 11.3% respiratory**. Susceptibility results cover **55 antibiotics**, plus "implied susceptibility" rules ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07664)). Structured documentation is consistent only from 2008, when Stanford adopted Epic. The cohort has a mean age of 56.7 years and is 66.9% female ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07664)). The tables carry what an order-time model needs. They include culture order mode and timing, ward (ICU, ED, inpatient, outpatient), prior antibiotic exposure by class and subtype, prior organisms and resistance, nursing-home visits in the 90 days before the culture, the Area Deprivation Index, Elixhauser and CCSR comorbidities, CPT-derived procedures, and labs and vitals summarised around the order ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07664); [Dryad](https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.jq2bvq8kp)). The authors do not plan regular updates. The descriptor reports **no ML baselines**, which leaves room for Empira to serve as a reference benchmark ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07664)).

Four items remain unverified. First, the licence: the Dryad page says only "licensed for reuse." Dryad's standard is CC0 1.0, but this was not confirmed on the record page. Second, the *Scientific Data* volume and article number. Third, the de-identification method. Fourth, the total count of susceptibility rows.

## One in six infections are resistant and empiric therapy misses one in five

AMR's global toll is large and growing. In 2019, bacterial AMR caused **1.27M attributable deaths** (95% UI 0.911–1.71M) and was associated with **4.95M deaths** across 204 countries. Six pathogens, led by *E. coli* and *S. aureus*, accounted for 929,000 of the attributable deaths ([IHME, Murray et al. Lancet 2022](https://www.healthdata.org/node/9472); [PubMed 35065702](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35065702/)). The print pages, 399(10325):629–655, are **UNVERIFIED**. The newer GBD 2021 analysis gives the latest observed figures: **1.14M attributable and 4.71M associated deaths in 2021**. It forecasts **1.91M attributable and 8.22M associated deaths in 2050** ([Naghavi et al. Lancet 2024;404:1199–1226, SGUL](https://openaccess.sgul.ac.uk/id/eprint/117151)). That forecast adds up to **more than 39M cumulative attributable deaths over 2025–2050**. Deaths in people aged 70 and over rose 89.7% from 1990 to 2021 ([IHME](https://healthdata.org/node/12106)), which matters for an adult academic cohort like ARMD's. WHO's GLASS 2025 report found that **1 in 6 lab-confirmed bacterial infections in 2023 were resistant**. Resistance rose in more than 40% of monitored pathogen–drug pairs between 2018 and 2023, by 5–15% a year. Globally, more than 40% of *E. coli* and more than 55% of *K. pneumoniae* isolates were resistant to third-generation cephalosporins ([WHO](https://www.who.int/news/item/13-10-2025-who-warns-of-widespread-resistance-to-common-antibiotics-worldwide)). In the US, the CDC's 2019 report remains the latest full burden estimate: **more than 2.8M resistant infections and more than 35,000 deaths a year**. Six hospital-onset resistant infections rose a combined 20% during COVID-19 ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/antimicrobial-resistance/data-research/threats/index.html)). CDC planned a new estimate for 2026, and whether it has been released is **UNVERIFIED**. Check before the pitch.

The empiric-therapy gap is the core of the pitch, and the evidence for it is strong. Across 13 US hospitals and 165,593 blood cultures, median time from collection was **0.80 days to Gram stain, 1.81 days to organism ID and 2.71 days to susceptibility** (IQR 2.46–2.99) ([Tabak et al. J Clin Microbiol 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6258864)). Clinicians choose drugs blind during that window, and they often miss. In 21,608 bloodstream-infection patients at 131 US hospitals, **19.3% received discordant empiric therapy**, which was associated with higher mortality. The paper reports this as aOR 1.37 (1.24–1.51) in its results and 1.5 in its abstract. The strongest predictor of discordance was a resistant phenotype, at **aOR 9.09** ([Kadri et al. Lancet Infect Dis 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7855478)). That last figure is the most direct justification for predicting resistance. In septic shock, about 20% of 5,715 patients received inappropriate initial therapy. Survival was **52.0% with appropriate therapy and 10.3% with inappropriate therapy** (aOR 8.99) ([Kumar et al. Chest 2009, PubMed 19696123](https://read.qxmd.com/read/19696123/initiation-of-inappropriate-antimicrobial-therapy-results-in-a-fivefold-reduction-of-survival-in-human-septic-shock)). The volume and DOI, 136(5):1237–48 and 10.1378/chest.09-0087, are **UNVERIFIED**. Each hour of delay in effective therapy was associated with a mean 7.6% rise in mortality ([Kumar Crit Care Med 2006, ClinCalc summary](https://clincalc.com/blog/2013/10/kumar-2006-antibiotic-delay-septic-mortality/)). The often-quoted "79.9% survival in the first hour" was not confirmed and should not be used.

Guidelines already use resistance thresholds and patient history, which is the logic Empira automates. IDSA/ESCMID's cystitis guideline endorses TMP-SMX "as long as local resistance patterns do not exceed 20%" ([ACEP Now review of Gupta et al. CID 2011;52:e103–e120](https://www.acepnow.com/article/clinical-guideline-review-acute-uncomplicated-cystitis/2/)). The DOI is **UNVERIFIED**, and so is the commonly cited 10% fluoroquinolone threshold for pyelonephritis. The IDSA 2025 complicated-UTI guideline asks clinicians to review urine cultures from the prior 3–6 months and to avoid agents a previous isolate resisted. It also says to avoid fluoroquinolones after exposure in the prior 12 months, and to pick agents with **at least 90% local susceptibility in septic shock and at least 80% in sepsis without shock** ([The Hospitalist, Apr 2026](https://www.the-hospitalist.org/hospitalist/article/40900/critical-care/whats-new-in-the-2025-idsa-updates-for-complicated-utis/)). The journal DOI is **UNVERIFIED**. That guideline is in effect a manual version of ARMD's prior-organism, prior-resistance and prior-exposure features. On overuse, **at least 30% of US outpatient antibiotic prescriptions are unnecessary**, and about half may be inappropriate once drug choice, dose and duration are counted ([CDC MMWR 2016;65(RR-6)](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/65/rr/rr6506a1.htm); [Fleming-Dutra JAMA 2016 via Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/infectious-disease/20160615/cdc-estimates-30-of-antibiotic-prescriptions-in-us-unnecessary)). The "47 million unnecessary prescriptions" figure appears only in secondary coverage ([MedicalResearch.com](https://medicalresearch.com/?p=23909)). The "half of hospital treatments inappropriate" claim traces to a Stanford webpage, not a peer-reviewed study. Treat both as weak.

In Corbin's Boston UTI replication of 15,806 infections, coverage was 90.4% vs 88.1% for clinicians, and the notes report that **93% of ciprofloxacin could shift to nitrofurantoin** ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9053259)). One note instead summarises this as "about 48% to TMP-SMX." Verify against the paper before quoting. Kanjilal's implied clinician inappropriate-therapy rate is about 12%, derived from 9.8% being an 18% reduction. That is close to Empira's 11.8% clinician baseline. But Empira includes all urine cultures, not only uncomplicated cystitis in women, and chooses from 28 drugs, so the two are not a head-to-head comparison. Older and adjacent work includes Oonsivilai 2018: 243 Cambodian children with bloodstream infection, ceftriaxone AUC 0.80 ([Oxford](https://www.tropicalmedicine.ox.ac.uk/publications/927681/modal)). It also includes Stracy et al., *Science* 2022, which optimised drug choice against post-treatment resistance emergence and cut predicted emergence by 48% ([Oxford Pathology](https://www.path.ox.ac.uk/news-article/antibiotic-prescribing-algorithm-reduces-the-risk-of-antibiotic-resistance-emergence-in-patients/)).

The frontier has moved past retrospective modelling. The Israeli "UTI Smart-Set" embedded an ML resistance forecast in an outpatient order set across 171,010 UTI diagnoses. Clinicians accepted its recommendations **66.0%** of the time. Mismatch was **8.9% when the recommendation was followed vs 14.2% when it was not**, and ciprofloxacin use was 6.4% vs 32.9% ([Shapiro Ben David et al. npj Digit Med 2025, DOI 10.1038/s41746-024-01400-5](https://library.kab.ac.ug/Record/doaj-art-879433afd04c4b459be0b61ee17a3a1d/Description)). The design is observational, followed vs not followed, so confounding is possible. The first registered prospective interventional study of a commercial ML empiric-therapy CDSS is **NCT07762378**. It tests iAST (Pragmatech AI Solutions) in 486 adults at Gregorio Marañón, Madrid, as a before/after study, not an RCT, planned for Sept 2026 to Sept 2027 ([registry mirror](https://tundraspace.com/directory/studies/nct07762378)). Recent EHR-only analogues include Indiana University's ESBL model on 132,955 cultures from 12 MGB hospitals. It reached AUROC 0.763, with NPV 95.8% at 90% sensitivity, and is not deployed ([arXiv 2609.05970](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05970)). An AIME 2025 study found a clinical LLM beat traditional ML for MRSA prediction in hospital-onset sepsis, AUC 0.73 vs 0.66 ([PubMed 40955356](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40955356/)). No published RCT of an EHR-based resistance-prediction CDSS was found, though the search was limited.

Compared with this prior art, much of Empira is not new. Gradient-boosted trees, temporal validation, comparison with clinicians' actual choices, a 20–40% reduction in urine mismatch, and Stanford-based personalized antibiograms all already exist. What Empira plausibly adds is five things. It models **28 antibiotics across three culture sites in one framework**, where prior work covered 4–12 agents and usually one site. It includes **respiratory cultures**, which are absent from the main prior papers. It is **reproducible on public ARMD**, unlike Yelin's data, which is available only under an MTA. Its **2022–2024 test window has a gap year**, which stresses drift after COVID. And it is set in a **US academic system with mixed care settings**. The weakness against the frontier is that Empira is retrospective only.

## Positioning: a predictive layer before the specimen exists

The timeline frames Empira's position. At order time (hour 0), only the chart exists. Point-of-care urine AST could answer within an hour, but it needs a device. Blood rapid AST answers 2–6 hours *after* a positive culture, which itself takes 1–2+ days. Routine susceptibility results arrive at about **2.7 days** ([Tabak 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6258864)). Empira therefore complements diagnostics rather than substituting for them. It informs the empiric choice that rapid AST later confirms or corrects. The faster urine tools, PA-100 and Pattern if cleared, are the only real timing threat, and they bring hardware, consumables and per-test cost that software avoids. In practice, Empira's competitor is the hospital's free, familiar static antibiogram plus local guidelines. The IDSA 2025 cUTI guideline's instruction to review prior cultures and exposures is the clinical hook: Empira does that review automatically and quantifies it ([The Hospitalist](https://www.the-hospitalist.org/hospitalist/article/40900/critical-care/whats-new-in-the-2025-idsa-updates-for-complicated-utis/)). Stewardship pharmacists and ID teams, who already buy tools like Sentri7, are the natural champions. The model is to integrate with their tools rather than replace them, as Sentri7 already did with DoseMeRx ([Wolters Kluwer](https://wolterskluwer.com/en/solutions/solesource/sentri7/antimicrobial-stewardship)).

The deck should be candid about three limits. First, discrimination is modest across the whole field: US EHR models reach AUROCs of 0.57–0.76 ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9053259); [arXiv 2609.05970](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05970)). Value should therefore be framed as coverage at clinician level plus narrower spectrum, not as accuracy alone. Second, models need local recalibration at each new site. The IU authors also warn that a negative prediction should not override escalation in ICU, sepsis or suspected bloodstream infection ([arXiv 2609.05970](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05970)). Third, a tool that recommends specific drugs may be regulated as device CDS. Prenosis's De Novo shows a pathway exists ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/04/03/prenosis-says-ai-tool-for-sepsis-approved-by-fda.html)). How FDA's 2022 CDS guidance applies to Empira is an **unverified inference**.

## Conclusion

The evidence supports a narrower, stronger claim than "AI predicts resistance." The modeling is proven and the commercial products are absent, so Empira's moat has to come from execution. That means calibration that transfers across sites, a public benchmark on ARMD that others can reproduce, multi-site breadth including respiratory cultures, and a credible path to prospective evidence. An observational deployment in Israel already exists, and a Spanish prospective study starts this autumn. "Retrospective only" will therefore read as behind the frontier within a year. A silent-mode run at Stanford, or external validation on ARMD-MGB, is the single most valuable next step for both the pitch and the technical reference.

Two facts deserve the most weight on a slide. Kadri's finding that a resistant phenotype raises the odds of discordant therapy **ninefold** links Empira's prediction target directly to mortality ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7855478)). Accelerate's collapse, about $718M in losses ending in a roughly $42M asset sale, is a sharp argument that software-only order-time prediction avoids the capital trap that has hurt rapid AST ([Elevenflo](https://elevenflo.com/blog/accelerate-diagnostics-bankruptcy)). Before external use, verify the Empira internal figures (8.0% vs 11.8%), the Corbin Boston ciprofloxacin-switch figure, Epic's model catalog and CDC's 2026 burden update.
